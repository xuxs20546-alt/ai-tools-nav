📖 Overview

Consensus is an AI search engine designed specifically for "scientific consensus" queries. It searches across 200+ million peer-reviewed academic papers, then uses AI to tell you—at the level of scientific evidence—what the consensus is on a given question. Its unique strength is that its answers don't rely on vague phrases like "it is widely believed" or "most experts agree"; instead, it presents the distribution of evidence with quantitative metrics—"75% of studies support this view (based on N=48 relevant papers), 12 of which are high-quality RCTs"—and you can view the citation information for all of these papers with one click.

Another standout feature of Consensus is its "instant citation" function—any sentence can be turned into a standard-format citation (APA, MLA, Chicago, etc.) with one click, ready to copy and paste into your paper. Consensus also supports search filtering—you can limit results to high-evidence-level research such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. This is extremely valuable for fields that require evidence-based decision-making (medicine, public health, education policy, etc.). The core search function is completely free, and the Pro plan adds unlimited searches, custom filters, bookmarks, and export features.

✨ Pros and Cons

✅ Advantages

  • "Consensus level" metric is unique and valuable: Instead of telling you "what the answer is," it shows "what the evidence supports"—a quantitative display of 75% support vs. 25% against makes you more objective.
  • Powerful evidence-quality filtering: Filter by evidence level—RCTs, systematic reviews, meta-analyses—an essential feature for evidence-based decision-making.
  • One-click citation generation: Every paper found can immediately generate APA/MLA/Chicago citations, extremely convenient for anyone writing a paper.

⚠️ Disadvantages

  • Only useful in fields with extensive academic research: Asking "what should I eat for breakfast" works well, but asking "which stock should I buy" is completely useless.
  • No coverage of Chinese-language papers: Also limited by database scope—content from Chinese academic journals cannot be found.
  • Overly academic responses: The answers are written for researchers—users without an academic background may be put off by the flood of jargon and statistical concepts.

🎯 Use Cases

  • Evidence-based medical decisions: Doctors or public health decision-makers quickly look up "the strength of evidence for a treatment's efficacy on a condition."
  • Literature search for academic writing: When writing an Introduction and needing to cite "the consensus of existing research in this field," Consensus provides a quantified evidence distribution.
  • Evidence base for policy research: Before deciding on a public health intervention, policymakers can quickly obtain a full picture of the academic evidence on the intervention's effectiveness.

💡 Usage Tips

  • Start with Yes/No questions: Consensus excels at binary questions like "is it effective"—queries such as "Does X increase Y" or "Is Z effective for W" yield the richest results.
  • Filter to RCTs before drawing conclusions: Without filtering, Consensus shows all kinds of evidence, including observational studies—which have far weaker causal inference power than RCTs.
  • High "consensus" ≠ absolute truth: Scientific consensus can change over time. High consensus (90%+) indicates strong current evidence, but never let consensus replace your own critical thinking.

Overview

Consensus was developed by a US startup, positioned as an "evidence-based AI search engine" focused on peer-reviewed academic literature. In August 2024, the company announced the completion of an $11.5 million Series A funding round led by Union Square Ventures, with funds used to expand the team and drive product development. It covers hundreds of millions of papers from sources such as PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and arXiv (officially around 200+ million). Unlike general-purpose chatbots, Consensus doesn't directly give "answers" but shows the distribution of research evidence.

This "evidence-first" design has earned it a stable user base in fields that emphasize evidence-based practice—medicine, public health, education policy—including researchers, doctors, and students.

Core Features

Consensus Meter. For questions like "is this intervention effective," a bar chart visually shows the proportion of studies supporting, opposing, or undecided on the conclusion—for example, "75% of studies support this view (based on 48 papers)"—while noting the number of high-quality RCTs among them.

Evidence-level filtering. Filter results by evidence type—RCTs, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, observational studies—helping users prioritize studies with stronger causal inference.

AI summaries and Copilot. Premium users can have AI synthesize multiple papers into a cited review-style answer, with every sentence traceable to specific literature, avoiding hallucination-style unverified claims.

One-click citation export. Any paper can quickly generate citations in standard formats such as APA, MLA, and Chicago, ready to paste directly into a paper or report—saving the time of manually compiling references.

Bookmarks and literature organization. The Premium plan supports saving search results and creating collections—ideal for long-term tracking of the latest evidence in a research area.

Getting Started

After registering a free account, type binary questions like "Does X improve Y" into the search box. At the top of the results page is the Consensus Meter's evidence distribution chart; below are cards of relevant papers, each labeled with journal, year, study type, and sample size.

Checking "show only RCTs / systematic reviews" quickly narrows the evidence; clicking a paper lets you read its abstract and generate citations. The typical path: ask a question → view the evidence distribution → filter for high-quality studies → export citations → cite them in your paper. The overall interaction is close to a normal search engine, requiring almost no learning curve.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths: The evidence distribution visualization is unique and objective, turning "what the research says" into readable quantitative metrics; evidence-level filtering is extremely valuable for evidence-based decisions; citation generation is standardized and hassle-free; the interface is ad-free with a pure academic atmosphere.

Limitations: It mainly covers English-language international journals, with essentially no coverage of Chinese papers; it's completely useless for non-academic questions like "which stock should I buy"; the free version limits searches and frequent use requires payment; answers are academic in tone, with a higher comprehension barrier for non-specialists.

Who It's For

  • Medical students, clinicians, and public health practitioners: quickly verify the strength of evidence for treatments;
  • Graduate students and researchers: systematic literature searching for literature reviews and Introductions;
  • Policy researchers: gathering the full evidence picture of intervention effectiveness for public decisions;
  • Science communicators and evidence-based content creators: verifying "whether a claim is supported by research."

FAQ

Q: How many papers does Consensus cover? A: Officially more than 200 million peer-reviewed academic papers; the specific database scope follows the official website.

Q: Can it search Chinese papers? A: Currently focused on international English-language journals, with limited coverage of Chinese journals—not suitable as a Chinese literature search tool for now.

Q: Is the free version enough? A: Fine for occasional queries; if you do literature searches daily and need export and bookmark features, consider upgrading to Premium.

Q: Are Consensus's conclusions trustworthy? A: It shows the distribution of evidence rather than personal opinions; credibility depends on the quality of the research itself, and you should still judge based on research design.
